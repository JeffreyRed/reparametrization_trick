01

Why do we need to sample inside a network?

In a Variational Autoencoder (VAE), the encoder produces a probability distribution over a latent space — not a single vector. At every forward pass, we sample a latent vector z from that distribution.

qφ(z | x) = 𝒩(z ; μφ(x), σ²φ(x))

The encoder outputs μ (mean) and σ (std dev). We then draw z ~ 𝒩(μ, σ²) and decode it to reconstruct x.

This seems fine — until we try to train with backpropagation. The loss depends on z, z is sampled stochastically, and you cannot backpropagate through a random sampling operation.

What we want

Gradients of the loss w.r.t. μ and σ — the encoder's outputs — so we can update the encoder network via SGD.

The problem

Sampling is a stochastic node. It has no well-defined gradient. The computation graph breaks here.

03

The Gradient Blockage Problem

Backpropagation needs a deterministic, differentiable computational path from the loss all the way back to the parameters. A stochastic node breaks this chain.

Naive forward graph:

θ (params)
→
μ, σ
→
sample
z ~ 𝒩(μ,σ²)
→
decoder
→
ℒ (loss)
Gradient flow is blocked at the stochastic node. ∂ℒ/∂μ and ∂ℒ/∂σ are undefined.

More formally, the objective is an expectation:

𝔼z~q(z|x)[ f(z) ]

We want to compute its gradient w.r.t. φ (the parameters that define the distribution). But the sampling distribution itself depends on φ, so naively:

∇φ 𝔼z~qφ(z|x)[ f(z) ] ≠ 𝔼z~qφ[ ∇φ f(z) ]

You can't just pull the gradient inside the expectation when the distribution depends on the parameter you're differentiating with respect to.

REINFORCE / Score Function

One workaround. Works but has very high variance — slow, noisy gradients.

Reparameterization

The elegant fix. Low variance, exact gradients, works beautifully in practice.

04

The Reparameterization Trick ✦

The key insight: separate the randomness from the parameters. Instead of sampling z directly from 𝒩(μ, σ²), sample a "raw" noise variable ε from a fixed standard normal, then deterministically transform it.

ε ~ 𝒩(0, 1) <-- sample from FIXED distribution
z = μ + σ · ε <-- deterministic transform

Now z still follows 𝒩(μ, σ²) — but the randomness lives in ε, which has no parameters. The path from μ and σ to z is now completely deterministic and differentiable!

θ (params)
→
μ, σ
→
z = μ + σε
→
decoder
→
ℒ
ε ~ 𝒩(0,1)
↗
Gradients now flow cleanly: ∂z/∂μ = 1 and ∂z/∂σ = ε

This means:

∂ℒ/∂μ = ∂ℒ/∂z · 1
∂ℒ/∂σ = ∂ℒ/∂z · ε

The gradient estimator becomes an expectation over the fixed distribution of ε — which we can now freely push through the expectation sign because ε doesn't depend on φ at all.

🎲

Noise is external

ε is sampled once, treated as a constant during backprop.

🔗

Deterministic path

μ → z and σ → z are simple arithmetic — fully differentiable.

📉

Low variance

Much lower variance than REINFORCE; training is stable and fast.

06

Putting it all Together: VAE

In a full VAE, the reparameterization trick enables end-to-end training of both the encoder and decoder jointly.

Full VAE forward pass:

1. x → Encoder → μφ(x), log σ²φ(x)
2. ε ~ 𝒩(0, I) <-- sample noise
3. z = μ + σ ⊙ ε <-- reparameterize
4. z → Decoder → x̂
5. ℒ = ||x - x̂||² + KL[ q(z|x) ‖ p(z) ]
The ELBO loss = reconstruction term + KL divergence regularizer
🔢

Reconstruction Loss

How well does the decoder reconstruct the input? (MSE or BCE)

📐

KL Divergence

Forces the latent space to be smooth and close to 𝒩(0,I).

⚡

End-to-End

Thanks to the trick, gradients flow through z back to the encoder.

The KL term has a closed form for Gaussians:

KL[ 𝒩(μ,σ²) ‖ 𝒩(0,1) ] = ½ · Σ ( 1 + log σ² - μ² - σ² )

This is analytically tractable — no sampling needed for the KL term. Only the reconstruction term requires a sample z, and that's where the trick is applied.
